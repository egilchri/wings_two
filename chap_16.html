Chapter IV

Chapter Summary & Character Study
Summary

The day after a dinner, Densher goes to Milly Theale’s hotel and asks for her. He finds her at home, and she appears glad to see him. When he asks about her health, she dismisses his concern, saying she is well. They talk about her impressions of London and their previous acquaintance in New York, but they do not mention Kate.

Densher asks about Milly's plans. She tells him she is going abroad for a few weeks but will return. They discuss her coming back to London, and she says she would return for Mrs. Lowder and for Kate.

A carriage arrives in the street below, and Milly says it is hers. She and Densher go to the balcony to look at it. She then invites him to join her for a drive. He accepts, and she leaves the room to get ready.

While Densher waits alone, a servant announces Kate Croy. Kate enters, sees Densher, and learns he is going for a drive with Milly. Kate says she will not wait for Milly. Densher tells Kate he is acting on her instigation. Kate suggests that he could leave before Milly returns and that she will make an excuse for him. Densher asks her to swear she loves him. He moves toward her, and she takes his hands to check him. He then agrees to do as she wishes. Kate tells him to be kind to Milly and leaves.

A few minutes later, Milly returns, dressed for the drive. Densher tells her that Kate visited but left upon finding him there. Milly accepts his explanation.

Character Study

This character study focuses on the characters' immediate states and the shifts they undergo within the text.

  • *Merton Densher**
    • Densher is presented as an analytical and self-aware man in a state of internal conflict. He arrives for his visit feeling "highly troubled" by a plan orchestrated by Kate and her aunt, which he initially views as a superfluous and potentially damaging manipulation. However, upon meeting Milly, his situation immediately shifts: his anxiety dissolves, replaced by his "old impression" of her ease.

    His central conflict quickly inverts. He came prepared to offer a contrived pity, but is struck by the realization that Milly, operating under a "beautiful delusion" created by Kate, now pities *him* for being supposedly unloved. This creates for him a "case of conscience." He feels trapped between the "indelicacy" of correcting her and the "betrayal" of Kate that doing so would entail. His decision to accept Milly's invitation for a drive is a decisive moment he sees as "rounding his corner," a conscious commitment to proceeding with the deception. His subsequent interaction with Kate reinforces this commitment, as her confident control overrides his lingering doubts and secures his loyalty.

  • *Milly Theale**
    • Initially, Milly is presented through Densher's anxious thoughts as "the poor girl," an object of a scheme and a potential invalid. Her situation shifts the moment she appears. In person, she is poised, graceful, and in command of the social interaction. She actively dispels any notion of being "one of the afflicted," using a "quick brave little art" to deflect concern for her health.

    Through Densher’s perspective, she is an active, if unwitting, participant in the unfolding drama. She is not a passive recipient of pity but is instead extending her own "benevolence" and "wasted charity" toward him. Her situation is also marked by an imminent change: she announces she is soon leaving London to go abroad for "high air," framing her departure as a strategic move to preserve her enjoyment of London for a future return.

  • *Kate Croy**
Kate is portrayed as the confident and decisive architect of the situation. Her sudden arrival does not cause her any surprise or "discomfiture." Her demeanor is one of "soft tranquillity" and control as she assesses the progress of her plan.

Her brief appearance serves as a pivotal moment that solidifies Densher’s wavering resolve. She expertly manages his anxiety, alternately challenging him to "break off" and reassuring him of her own position. She operates with handsome consistency, treating the success of her "instigation" as a pleasantry. Her situation is that of a director visiting a set: she checks on the primary actor, reinforces his motivation, gives him final instructions—"be kind to her... Leave the rest to her"—and makes a swift, decisive exit.
