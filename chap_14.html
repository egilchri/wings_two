Chapter II

Chapter Summary & Character Study
Summary

A man, Densher, receives a telegram from Mrs. Lowder inviting him to dinner. He arrives at her house and finds her alone. Mrs. Stringham enters, also by herself, followed shortly by Kate. Mrs. Lowder looks Kate over from head to foot. Two more male guests arrive.

Mrs. Stringham explains that her companion, Milly Theale, is unwell and could not come. After dinner, Kate and Densher speak privately for ten minutes about Milly's illness. During the dinner, the main topic of conversation had been Milly's social success. The guests, including Mrs. Lowder and Kate, appeal to Densher as someone who knew her first. Mrs. Lowder asks if Milly was less prominent in her own country, and a guest theorizes that London society discovered her. Mrs. Stringham interjects that Milly was a great success in Boston, then tells Densher he knows nothing about her friend.

Densher defends himself, stating Milly was kind to him in New York and notes that Mrs. Stringham was not there at the time. Mrs. Lowder and Kate tease them about this. Densher and Mrs. Stringham agree that neither of them was with Milly for very long. Densher states he never "chattered" about her, which pleases Mrs. Stringham. Mrs. Lowder suggests his silence proves how deep his impression of Milly was. Densher insists he is not an authority on Milly.

Kate supports Densher, saying Milly is not easy to know. She tells him that the group has "developed" Milly since he last saw her, and she is not the same person. Mrs. Stringham disagrees, stating Milly is incapable of change. Kate concludes by telling Densher he must now accept them all on trust. He agrees, and so does Mrs. Stringham.

It wasn't a case for pedantry; when people were at her pass everything was allowed. And her pass was now, as by the sharp click of a spring, just completely his own—to the extent, as he felt, of her deep dependence on him. Anything he should do or shouldn't would have close reference to her life, which was thus absolutely in his hands—and ought never to have reference to anything else. It was on the cards for him that he might kill her—that was the way he read the cards as he sat in his customary corner. The fear in this thought made him let everything go, kept him there actually, all motionless, for three hours on end. He renewed his consumption and smoked more cigarettes than he had ever done in the time. What had come out for him had come out, with this first intensity, as a terror; so that action itself, of any sort, the right as well as the wrong—if the difference even survived—had heard in it a vivid "Hush!" the injunction to keep from that moment intensely still. He thought in fact while his vigil lasted of several different ways for his doing so, and the hour might have served him as a lesson in going on tiptoe.

After leaving her, he didn't go home because he didn't want to. What he finally took home, when he ventured to leave the place, was the perceived truth that he might on any other system go straight to destruction. He was aware when he walked away again that even Fleet Street wouldn't at this juncture successfully touch him. His manager might wire that he was wanted, but he could easily be deaf to his manager. His money for the idle life might be none too much; happily, however, Venice was cheap, and it was moreover the queer fact that Milly in a manner supported him. He should be able to be still enough through everything.

When he left, he knew that even his work in Fleet Street wouldn't affect him. His boss could send him a telegram asking him to come back, but he could ignore it. He might not have much money for a life of leisure, but fortunately, Venice was inexpensive, and it was also strange that Milly in a sense supported him.
